How this is calculated

The International Labour Organization (ILO) publishes yearly averages of how many people are employed and unemployed in every country, built so that countries can be compared. Its latest edition already includes its own estimates for and , so this page does not project the data itself.

Each yearly average is placed at the middle of its year, and the level at any moment is a straight line between neighbouring years:

employed(now)   = line through the yearly averages at mid-year
net new jobs    = employed(now) - employed(1 January)
unemployed(now) = line through the yearly averages at mid-year

Net new jobs means jobs gained minus jobs lost. Far more jobs are created and destroyed every year than this number shows, but no source counts that gross churn for the whole world. A negative number means fewer people are in work than at the start of the year, often because the working-age population is shrinking rather than because of layoffs. Real employment also moves with seasons and the economy, so the live counters are a model, not a measurement. Germany is shown separately but is also part of the EU.

What these numbers mean

Who counts as unemployed

Under the international definition, you are unemployed if you have no job, are available to work, and have actively looked for work recently. Working even one hour in a week counts as employed.

Who is missing from that number

People who want a job but have given up looking, or can't start right away (for example because of caring duties), are not counted as unemployed. The ILO's wider "jobs gap" includes them and is more than twice as large as unemployment.

Having a job is also not the same as a good job. Most workers in the world are in informal work, without a contract or social protection, and many more work fewer hours than they would like.

Why net new jobs matter

The world's working-age population keeps growing, so the economy has to create millions of jobs a year just to stand still. When net new jobs grow faster than the labour force, unemployment falls; when they don't, it rises.

💡 Think of an escalator going down: new people step onto the labour market every year, and job creation has to outpace them just to stay in the same place.

Why rich countries can show "jobs lost"

In ageing countries such as Japan, more people retire than enter working age. Employment can then fall even while unemployment stays low and employers struggle to hire.

Sources: ILO, World Employment and Social Outlook: Trends; ILOSTAT concepts and definitions.
